*The Kreutzer Sonata* was a nice, smooth read, as one would expect from Tolstoy. The book is fairly short and direct: at no point does it feel as if the author is stalling the story, the pace flows naturally, and the plot has a clear structure. Unfortunately, I do not find it to be on the same level as Tolstoy's other books, which is certainly a high bar to clear.

At some points, the book reads more like a manifesto than a novel. Pozdnyshev proselytizes for his worldview as he tells his story and the narrator barely interacts with him. On the few occasions the narrator attempts to challenge Pozdnyshev, he is quickly shut down. Considering the extreme views Tolstoy developed later in life along with his known unhappy marriage, it feels as if Pozdnyshev is an author insert.

However, the novel makes sure to mock Pozdnyshev: for most of it, he is depicted as all bark and no bite. Despite his views, he still succumbs to his sexual desires. Despite his unhappy marriage, he still blames the musician, Troukhatchevsky, for his marital issues. Not only this, but Pozdnyshev is portrayed as a physically weird man who has trouble interacting with others. And, in the end, despite trying to justify the murder of his wife, he regrets it and seeks forgiveness from the narrator and, indirectly, from the reader.

The question that remained with me at the end of the book is: does Tolstoy feel guilty for his unhappy marriage, or is this book a way of exposing his worldview? This certainly reads as the story of a regretful man but, at the same time, Tolstoy is not Pozdnyshev. Tolstoy did not murder his wife despite his unhappy marriage. Even so, Pozdnyshev rejects any pushback and discussion and, without these challenges, he fails to grow and move on, making any regret shallow and pointless.
